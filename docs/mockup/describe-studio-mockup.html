Is audio description needed?

The app compared what is shown with what is said. Audio description is needed only where important visual information is not already in the audio. You make the final call on every segment.

The rule this check applies

W3C states that if all important information in the video track is already conveyed in the audio track, no additional audio description is necessary. The app never marks a whole video as exempt by itself. It reports segment by segment and records your decision with your name and the date.

Standards checked: WCAG 2.1 and 2.2 (1.2.3, 1.2.5, 1.2.7, 1.2.8), Section 508, ADA Title II web rule, EN 301 549, DCMP Description Key.

    Settings

    What live mode is for

    It adds access where there would otherwise be none. It does not replace a human captioner (CART) when one is a student's approved accommodation, and its error rate is higher for some accents and speech patterns than others.

    Description standards

    Ten criteria for audio description, also called video description. Each one comes from an organization of people with disabilities, quoted and cited. The app checks its drafts against these, and this page ships inside the app.

    1. 1. Describe what is essential first

      Choose the visual information a viewer needs to understand and appreciate the content. Leave the rest.

      “Describe what is most essential for the viewer to know in order to understand and appreciate the image.”

      Source: American Council of the Blind, Audio Description Project (2009)

      In this app: The need check ranks visual information by whether the audio already carries it, and step 2 asks you what viewers need from the video.

    2. 2. Say what you see, without interpreting

      Describe what is visible. Do not add opinion, guess at feelings, or leave things out because they are uncomfortable.

      “What you see is what you say.”

      Source: American Council of the Blind, Audio Description Project (2009). Also Described and Captioned Media Program (n.d.): “Describe objectively without personal interpretation, censorship, or comment.”

      In this app: Drafting prompts forbid inferred emotions and motives. A draft that says “he is furious” is flagged; “he clenches his fist” is not.

    3. 3. Be accurate

      A wrong description is worse than none, because the listener cannot check it against the screen.

      “There must be no errors in word selection, pronunciation, diction, or enunciation.”

      Source: Described and Captioned Media Program (n.d.)

      In this app: A second pass checks every draft against the frames and the text read from the screen, and flags anything it cannot confirm.

    4. 4. Respect the soundtrack

      Fit descriptions into pauses. Do not talk over dialogue or important sound, and do not fill every silence.

      “Do not be tempted to fill every pause. Allow the atmosphere and background noise to come through.”

      Source: Audio Description International Guidelines Committee (2003), published by the ACB Audio Description Project

      In this app: Descriptions are timed to gaps in speech. When one does not fit, you choose to shorten it or use extended description.

    5. 5. Use clear, concise language for this audience

      Write so the description is understood the first time it is heard, at a level that fits the viewers.

      “Write simply, clearly and concisely. Ensure your description is easy to comprehend the first time it is heard.”

      Source: Audio Description International Guidelines Committee (2003). Also Described and Captioned Media Program (n.d.) on age-appropriate vocabulary and pace.

      In this app: Your audience answer in step 2 sets vocabulary and length. Concise is the default.

    6. 6. Describe people equitably

      Describe visible characteristics for everyone or for no one. Never treat one group as the default, and never guess identity.

      “Citing the race only of non-white individuals establishes ‘white’ as a default and is unacceptable.”

      Source: American Council of the Blind, Audio Description Project (2009). Also Described and Captioned Media Program (n.d.): “Do not make assumptions as to someone’s race.”

      In this app: The app never infers race, gender, age, or disability. Names and self-descriptions come only from you or from text on screen.

    7. 7. Give equal access to meaning, at the same moment

      The point is the meaning and intent of the program, delivered when sighted viewers get it. In teaching, describe before the content, not after.

      “When expanded description is a technical option, provide description before the content rather than after.”

      Source: Described and Captioned Media Program (n.d.)

      In this app: The “Extended, described first” style in step 1 does this. The exported player supports the pause.

    8. 8. Treat the voice as part of access

      A human voice is the standard. A synthetic voice is acceptable only when it is natural, clear, correctly pronounced, and listeners are told.

      “Human-voiced Audio Description remains the gold standard.”

      Source: American Council of the Blind (n.d.), Guidelines for the Use of Text to Speech

      In this app: Every export and the player state that the voice is synthetic. You can replace any cue with your own recorded voice.

    9. 9. Put blind and low vision people in the review

      Quality is judged by the people description is for. Machine drafts are never released unreviewed.

      “Get feedback.”

      Source: American Council of the Blind, Audio Description Project (2009). The ACB text-to-speech guidelines also call for quality review that includes blind and low-vision professionals.

      In this app: The review editor works by keyboard and screen reader, and the record names who reviewed. Quick drafts are stamped “not reviewed by a person.”

    10. 10. Protect the images people share

      Images collected to produce descriptions should not be stored, passed on, or misused.

      “Implement sensible protections that prevent images taken, imported, or otherwise collected for the purpose of providing visual descriptions to the blind from being stored, disseminated, or otherwise misused.”

      Source: National Federation of the Blind, Resolution 2024-08 (2024)

      In this app: Processing happens on your computer by default. Cloud use is off until you turn it on, and you see what would be sent first.

    OrganizationWho leads itHow it is used here
    American Council of the Blind (ACB), including its Audio Description ProjectBlind-led. A membership organization of people who are blind or have low vision. Its constitution states that all members of the Board of Directors shall be legally blind.Primary source for criteria 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9.
    National Federation of the Blind (NFB)Blind-led. “We are blind people, from our democratically elected leaders to our diverse nationwide membership.”Primary source for criterion 10.
    Described and Captioned Media Program (DCMP)Administered by the National Association of the Deaf, an organization “of, by and for deaf and hard of hearing individuals.” Deaf-led, not blind-led. The Description Key was developed with the American Foundation for the Blind.Primary source for criteria 3 and 7. Supporting source for 2, 5, and 6.
    Audio Description International (ADI)Leadership not confirmed. Its 2003 guidelines are published by the ACB Audio Description Project.Quoted in criteria 4 and 5 because ACB publishes them.
